MACHINE LEARNING

ALGORITHM

LEARNING OUTCOMES

1: Articulate the legal, social, ethical, and professional issues faced by machine learning professionals.
2: Understand the applicability and challenges associated with different datasets for the use of machine learning algorithms.
3: Apply and critically appraise machine learning techniques to real-world problems, particularly where technical risk and uncertainty is involved.
4: Systematically develop and implement the skills required to be effective member of a development team in a virtual professional environment, adopting real-life perspectives on team roles and organisation.

ARTEFACTS

Weekly tasks, coding exercises, and machine learning experiments.

REFLECTIONS

Unit 1
This project deepened my understanding of how Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 shape modern healthcare, particularly through machine learning and digital integration. While I initially focused on technical vulnerabilities, peer feedback and further reading helped me recognise that cybersecurity failures often stem from governance, oversight, and organisational behaviour. This realisation shifted my perspective toward Industry 5.0’s human‑centred resilience, where the priority is maintaining safe, continuous patient care even during digital disruption. Through collaborative discussion, critical self‑analysis, and engagement with literature, I developed stronger analytical skills, a more nuanced understanding of ethical and professional responsibilities, and a clearer appreciation of how machine learning practitioners must balance innovation with robust security and governance. My contributions and reflections demonstrate meaningful learning that I can apply to real‑world data science and healthcare contexts.
Reflecting on the discussion of the 4th Industrial Revolution, I am increasingly aware of how deeply machine learning and digital technologies are reshaping healthcare—and how unprepared many organisations still are for the risks that accompany this transformation. The WannaCry attack on the NHS was a reminder that technological progress can quickly become a liability when governance, patching, and oversight fall behind. Peer insights on network segmentation, secure backups, and zero‑trust approaches made me think more critically about the practical skills and responsibilities required of anyone working with machine learning systems. Géron’s introduction reinforced this by showing that machine learning is not only about building models but understanding their limitations, vulnerabilities, and ethical implications. This has made me reflect on my own development: becoming proficient in machine learning means learning to anticipate failure, design for resilience, and ensure that intelligent systems ultimately protect people rather than expose them to harm.

Unit 2
Exploratory data analysis on the auto‑mpg dataset strengthened my ability to understand and prepare real‑world data for machine learning. Identifying missing values, examining skewness and kurtosis, and interpreting correlation and scatter‑plot relationships helped me recognise how data quality and distribution shape model behaviour. The strong negative correlation between mpg and weight, along with visible patterns in the pair plot, improved my understanding of feature interactions and their implications for predictive modelling. Converting categorical values to numerical form further prepared the dataset for algorithmic use. This work also highlighted ethical responsibilities around data accuracy, transparency, and bias when handling real‑world datasets. Collaborating in a virtual team environment enhanced my communication and problem‑solving skills, reinforcing the importance of shared understanding during technical tasks. Overall, Unit 2 deepened my confidence in applying EDA techniques and critically evaluating dataset suitability under conditions of uncertainty.

Unit 3
Unit 3 strengthened my theoretical and practical understanding of correlation and regression by applying these techniques to real datasets. Computing covariance and Pearson’s correlation helped me interpret relationships between variables, such as the strong positive correlation (0.979) observed in the covariance_pearson_correlation exercise. Implementing simple linear regression allowed me to predict values, including estimating y = 85.59 when x = 10, which demonstrated how regression models support real‑world decision‑making. Multiple linear regression further expanded my skills by incorporating several predictors, as shown in the CO₂ prediction example. Polynomial regression highlighted how non‑linear patterns can be modelled effectively, reinforcing the importance of selecting appropriate techniques for different datasets. These activities improved my confidence in analysing data, interpreting statistical outputs, and understanding how machine learning models behave under uncertainty. Overall, Unit 3 deepened my ability to apply correlation and regression meaningfully in practical scenarios.

Unit 4
Unit 4 strengthened my ability to apply linear regression techniques to complex, real‑world datasets using Scikit‑Learn. Working with global GDP and population data required careful preprocessing, correlation analysis, and model evaluation. The weak Pearson correlation coefficient (‑0.10) and non‑significant p‑value highlighted the importance of statistically validating relationships before modelling. Implementing linear regression and interpreting the intercept (15377.0598) and slope (‑0.000003) improved my understanding of how small coefficients can reflect minimal predictive influence. This unit also emphasised ethical responsibilities, particularly when modelling socio‑economic data where misinterpretation can lead to misleading policy insights. Collaborating in a virtual team environment enhanced my communication and problem‑solving skills, especially when aligning modelling decisions and interpreting outputs collectively. Overall, Unit 4 deepened my confidence in Scikit‑Learn, regression evaluation, and responsible use of global datasets under uncertainty.

Unit 5
Unit 5 deepened my understanding of clustering by connecting mathematical similarity measures with practical algorithm behaviour. Calculating Jaccard similarity and distance for Jack, Mary, and Jim strengthened my ability to evaluate categorical data and understand how similarity metrics influence clustering outcomes. The K‑Means wiki activity clarified the iterative logic behind clustering: point reassignment based on nearest centroids and centroid updates based on group means. Seeing these animations helped me appreciate convergence, initialization sensitivity, and the limitations of Euclidean‑distance‑based clustering, especially for irregular cluster shapes. This unit also highlighted key pitfalls, such as biased cluster boundaries and misleading groupings when data is poorly scaled or non‑spherical. Reflecting on these challenges improved my ability to critically assess clustering results and recognise when alternative methods may be more appropriate. Overall, Unit 5 strengthened both my theoretical understanding and practical evaluation skills in unsupervised learning.

Unit 6
Unit 6 strengthened my ability to apply clustering techniques to large, real world datasets using Python. Working with iris, wine, and weatherAUS datasets helped me understand how scaling, feature selection, and distance metrics influence cluster formation and interpretability. Implementing K Means reinforced the importance of evaluating cluster quality through inertia, silhouette scores, and visual diagnostics. The development team project extended this learning by applying K Prototypes to mixed Airbnb data, highlighting practical challenges such as initialization sensitivity, cluster stability, and the need for meaningful business interpretation. Lecturer feedback emphasised that technical clustering outputs alone are insufficient; clear articulation of business value and critical evaluation are essential. This insight improved my appreciation of how clustering supports decision making beyond numerical segmentation. Overall, Unit 6 enhanced my technical proficiency, deepened my understanding of clustering pitfalls, and strengthened my ability to interpret and communicate results within a professional analytical context.

Unit 7
Unit 7 strengthened my understanding of artificial neural networks by moving from simple perceptrons to multi layer architectures. Implementing the basic perceptron clarified how weighted sums and activation thresholds produce binary decisions, as shown in the AND operator exercise where only the input [1,1] produced an output of 1. Extending this to a multi layer perceptron using the sigmoid function demonstrated how non linear transformations allow networks to model more complex patterns, with outputs varying across input combinations. These practical exercises helped me appreciate how network structure, activation choice, and weight initialization influence model behaviour. The unit also highlighted the risks and uncertainties of ANN deployment, including overfitting, interpretability challenges, and sensitivity to training data. Working collaboratively reinforced the importance of clear communication when designing and debugging neural models. Overall, Unit 7 deepened both my theoretical understanding and practical confidence in applying ANN techniques to real world problems.

Unit 8
Unit 8 strengthened my understanding of how artificial neural networks learn through error minimisation, particularly through implementing gradient descent and observing how learning rate and iteration choices affect convergence. This technical work aligned closely with Collaborative Discussion 2, which deepened my appreciation of the legal and ethical responsibilities surrounding ANN‑assisted writing. My initial post highlighted efficiency and accessibility benefits, while peers emphasised accountability, cognitive engagement, and risks to creative diversity. John‑Jon’s focus on procedural oversight and Buçinca et al.’s (2021) findings on cognitive‑forcing functions clarified that responsible AI use requires structured human verification rather than passive acceptance. Sarah’s analogy to calculators (Richardson, 2018) helped contextualise concerns about skill decline, suggesting that AI can enhance capability when used deliberately. Together, these insights reinforced that ANN development and deployment demand both technical competence and sustained human judgment to ensure accuracy, integrity, and meaningful creativity.

Unit 9
Unit 9 introduced the foundations of convolutional neural networks and strengthened my understanding of how computer vision models extract structure from images. Working through the CNN object‑recognition notebook helped me see how convolutional layers detect edges and textures, how pooling reduces spatial complexity, and how deeper layers progressively learn more abstract features. This practical exercise clarified why CNNs outperform traditional machine‑learning approaches on visual tasks: they learn hierarchical representations directly from pixel data rather than relying on manual feature engineering. The activity also highlighted the importance of dataset quality, preprocessing, and architectural choices when training vision models. Reflecting on emerging research underscored both the power and risks of CNNs, including bias amplification and misclassification in safety‑critical domains. Overall, Unit 9 improved my ability to undertake basic computer‑vision tasks, interpret CNN behaviour, and appreciate the broader significance of convolutional architectures in modern AI applications.
